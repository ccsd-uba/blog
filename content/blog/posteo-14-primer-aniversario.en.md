On Friday, November 20, we gathered at FIUBA (the School of Engineering of the University of Buenos Aires) with faculty, researchers and colleagues from several institutions to celebrate the first year of the Center for Cryptography and Distributed Systems. The meeting was a chance to take stock of the year and to start shaping the 2027 agenda together.

![Attendees seated at desks in a FIUBA classroom, listening to Diego Kingston, Sebastián Civallero and Pablo Deymonnaz speaking at the front of the room](/images/aniversario-ccsd-sala.jpg)

## Why a center like CCSD

The conversation began with the reason the Center was created. Until a year ago, the university had no interdisciplinary space devoted to in-depth study of cryptography, cybersecurity, critical infrastructure and distributed systems. In recent months these topics have taken a central place on the international agenda, which reinforces the need for a dedicated place to research and teach them.

## Quantum computing and cryptography

A sufficiently powerful quantum computer could break elliptic curve cryptography, which today protects everything from web communications to cryptocurrencies. In March, Google Quantum AI published new estimates that lower the resources needed to do so. Instead of publishing the circuits, the team presented a zero-knowledge proof that lets others verify the result without revealing how it is achieved, a decision that sparked a lot of discussion in the community.

At the breakfast we also talked about the timeline of the post-quantum transition. Many systems, such as satellites, rely on technology that is hard to update, so the change will take years. Several countries already have plans underway, starting with infrastructure tied to national security and then moving on to citizens' data.

## Artificial intelligence and verification

Since late 2025, AI models have been able to solve complex tasks with considerable autonomy. This lets small teams make progress on timelines that were unthinkable before, but it also produces a volume of code that no person can fully review, and that review is increasingly left to other AIs.

In this context, formal verification, which means mathematically proving that a program does what it is supposed to do, becomes much more relevant. For years it was too costly for most projects. Today AI itself makes it accessible, and it offers stronger guarantees than traditional testing. Human involvement is still necessary, although its role is changing.

With both quantum computing and AI, the idea that came up is that we can no longer take for granted the foundations we build on.

## The first year

In 2026 we taught the Center's first seminar, on Coding Theory, Low-Degree Testing and PIOPs, and formed a study group that carried it forward. We presented papers at conferences, including CommitLLM, a protocol for verifying language model inference. We gave outreach talks for students, started two PIDAE projects and launched a doctoral and postdoctoral fellowship program.

## Toward 2027

What we discussed at the breakfast confirms that the Center's work is only just beginning. Next year we want to expand our training offering, with new editions of the seminar and other courses, and strengthen our ties with institutions that have long worked on these topics. Along those lines, we are working on agreements with other universities and on organizing a conference with international speakers, intended as a reference forum in the region for discussing cryptography and distributed systems.

Thank you to everyone who joined us during this first year. We look forward to seeing you in what comes next.
